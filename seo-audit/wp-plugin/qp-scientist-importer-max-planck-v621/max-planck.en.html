Max Planck Biography | Father of Quantum Theory

Author: Qpedia Editorial — Reviewed by PhD Theoretical Physics (Sharif) — Published: 2026-09-21 — Updated: 2026-09-21 — About | Contact
Max Planck was a conservative professor who accidentally split physics in two. He did not want to quantize the world, but when the blackbody data refused to fit any classical formula, he dared to say energy comes in packets. That single sentence on 14 December 1900 became the birthday of quantum physics and later made possible every modern technology from transistors to MRI.

Childhood in Kiel: A House of Law and Theology

Max Planck was born on 23 April 1858 in the port city of Kiel. His family belonged to the educated North German elite; his father Johann Julius Wilhelm Planck was a professor of law at Kiel and later Munich, and both his grandfather and great-grandfather were theology professors in Göttingen. This background meant three things were always present in the Planck household: respect for law, Protestant discipline, and love for music. Planck later wrote that his family was known for integrity, duty, and generosity, values that stayed with him for life.

Sources: Britannica – Max Planck biography | Stanford Encyclopedia – Planck | Nobel 1918 biographical

When Max was 9, the family moved to Munich and he entered the Maximilians Gymnasium, one of Bavaria’s strictest schools. There a teacher named Hermann Müller explained the conservation of energy with such passion that Planck later said it was the moment he knew physics must be his profession. He was a quiet, extremely orderly student, got excellent grades, played piano at concert level, and for a while hesitated between music and physics. He finally chose physics because, as he put it, music could be loved as a friend, but physics had to be lived as destiny.

Sources: Britannica – early life | Stanford – childhood

An interesting detail about his name is that he was registered as Marx, a common family name, but from around age ten he signed himself Max and everyone accepted it. This small change shows he wanted a simpler, independent identity from childhood, something later visible in his scientific style: simplifying complex problems down to a universal constant.

Source: Stanford – name Marx to Max

Education: From Munich to Berlin and Disappointment with Great Professors

Planck entered the University of Munich in autumn 1874. He expected inspiration from Philipp von Jolly, a famous physicist of the time, but Jolly told him physics was almost complete and only a few small gaps remained, not worth pursuing. This sentence, later legendary in textbooks, did not discourage Planck; on the contrary, it pushed him toward thermodynamics, a branch he felt contained the most fundamental laws of nature. He read Rudolf Clausius’s books on his own, obsessively, and fell in love with the concept of entropy.

Sources: Britannica – Munich University | Stanford – thermodynamics

For variety, he spent the academic year 1877-1878 in Berlin, where Hermann von Helmholtz and Gustav Kirchhoff taught. He hoped to find a spark in Germany’s scientific capital, but later wrote he found their lectures dry and uninspiring. However, this period taught him that real progress in physics comes not from flashy lectures but from quiet, precise work on fundamental problems. He received his doctorate in July 1879 in Munich, at age 21, with a thesis on the second law of thermodynamics, and completed his habilitation a year later in the same city.

Sources: Britannica – Berlin and doctorate 1879 | Stanford – PhD 1879

His career path was slow but steady: associate professor in Kiel in 1885, successor to Kirchhoff in Berlin in 1889, where he remained until retirement in 1928. In Berlin a circle of brilliant students gathered around him, including Max von Laue, Walther Nernst, and later Werner Heisenberg and Wolfgang Pauli, both future Nobel laureates. Planck was a strict but very supportive teacher and was among the first to take Einstein’s special relativity in 1905 seriously, organizing a colloquium for him.

Sources: Britannica – professor Kiel 1885 Berlin 1889 | Stanford – mentorship

The Boiling Point: When Blackbody Split Physics

The 1890s were for Planck a decade of obsession with one problem: blackbody radiation. Laboratories in Berlin led by Heinrich Rubens and Ferdinand Kurlbaum had measured the most precise spectral data, showing Wien’s law failed at long wavelengths, while Rayleigh-Jeans law diverged to infinity at short wavelengths, later called the ultraviolet catastrophe. Planck, conservative by character and averse to unjustified leaps, spent months trying to build an interpolation formula from classical thermodynamics that would cover both limits.

Sources: Klein 1962 – Max Planck and beginnings of quantum theory | Britannica – ultraviolet catastrophe

On 19 October 1900 he presented his famous formula to the German Physical Society: a formula that matched Rubens’s data with stunning accuracy but had no theoretical derivation. Planck knew this was not enough. By his own account, the following weeks were “the hardest work of my life” to find a basis for the formula. Finally, on 14 December 1900, in the same society, he proposed an idea he himself called “an act of desperation”: to obtain the correct law, one must assume the energy of oscillators inside the blackbody cannot have any value, but must be an integer multiple of a basic amount, E = n h ν. This h was a new constant, extremely small, later named Planck’s constant. That day, without Planck wanting it, quantum physics was born.

Sources: Planck 1901 – distribution law Annalen | Klein 1962 – 14 Dec 1900 | Nobel – birth of quantum 1900

Three Achievements that Changed the World

1. Planck’s Radiation Law and Solving the Ultraviolet Catastrophe

Planck’s law says the spectral energy density at frequency ν and temperature T is u(ν,T) = (8πhν³/c³)/(exp(hν/kT)-1). This simple formula reaches Rayleigh-Jeans at low frequencies and Wien at high frequencies and avoids infinite divergence. Its philosophical importance was that for the first time it showed nature at small scales is discrete, not continuous. Planck himself for years saw this discreteness only as a mathematical trick and hoped it could be returned to classical physics, but Einstein in 1905 with the photoelectric effect showed that light itself is quantized and there was no way back.

Sources: Planck 1901 Annalen | Britannica – Planck's law | Einstein 1905 photoelectric

2. Planck Constant h — Smallest Possible Action

Planck’s constant h is today one of seven defining constants of the SI system, exactly 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s. This number is the quantum of action, the smallest amount of action nature allows. In his first estimate in 1900 Planck obtained about 6.55×10⁻²⁷ erg·s, only a few percent off the modern value, and simultaneously calculated Boltzmann’s constant k with good accuracy. He then built Planck natural units from h, speed of light c, and gravitational constant G; units today used in quantum cosmology and string theory as fundamental scales of the universe.

Sources: CODATA 2019 – Planck constant exact | Britannica – Planck constant | Klein 1962 – first estimate

3. Teacher of Generations and Champion of Relativity

Perhaps few know that without Planck’s support, Einstein might have stayed years in the patent office. From 1906 onward Planck taught relativity in Berlin seminars, in 1913 went personally with Nernst to Zurich to convince Einstein to take a Berlin professorship, and in 1914 made him a member of the Prussian Academy. Between 1913-1914 he was rector of Berlin University and 1930-1937 president of Kaiser Wilhelm Society (today Max Planck Society); an institution today with 83 institutes, Germany’s largest basic research organization. His direct students like Heisenberg, Pauli, and von Laue each shook 20th-century physics.

Behind Equations: Love, Faith, and Repeated Tragedies

In 1887 Planck married Marie Merck, daughter of a Munich banker, and had four children: Karl (1888), twins Emma and Grete (1889), and Erwin (1893). His family life was initially calm but from 1909 onward turned into endless tragedy. Marie died in 1909 probably from tuberculosis, Karl was killed in 1916 at the Battle of Verdun in World War I, Grete died in 1917 during first childbirth and Emma in 1919 the same way. Planck married Marga von Hoesslin in 1911 and had a son Hermann (1911-1954), but the shadow of death remained with him.

Sources: Britannica – family | Cantor's Paradise – personal tragedies

Personality-wise, everyone knew him for obsessive integrity, loyalty, and love for music. He played piano daily, performed violin-piano duets with Einstein, and believed music shows cosmic order to humans. His students said he lectured very precisely and slowly, never missed a class, and even in hardest years of World War I did not cancel his courses. This sense of duty later cost him dearly in Nazi era.

Source: Britannica – character music

Faith and Doubt: From Church Elder to 1947 Letter

Planck grew up Lutheran and for years served as church elder (Kirchenältester) in Berlin, a role like assistant pastor in social affairs. In his famous lecture Religion and Natural Science in 1937 he said: “There can never be any real opposition between religion and science, for the one is the complement of the other.” This sentence was later quoted widely. But in a private letter on 18 June 1947 to his friend Kick he wrote: “I am deeply religious, but I do not believe in a personal Christian God.” Historians today see him more as a deist; someone who believed in moral order of world, but not in a God who intervenes in daily prayers.

Sources: MPG Archive – religion letter 1947 | Enlightened Crowd – church elder | Wikiquote – 1937 lecture

Between Duty and Conscience: 1933-1945

When Hitler came to power in January 1933, many scientists left Germany, but 75-year-old Planck stayed. He thought if he stayed he could protect German science from inside. In spring 1933 he personally met Hitler to plead for Jewish colleagues like Fritz Haber and Einstein. The meeting was disastrous; Hitler became angry and said Jews are all communists. Planck later wrote that at that moment he realized logic did not work with that regime. Nevertheless he publicly criticized expulsions, resigned from Kaiser Wilhelm Society, and in 1937 was forced to resign under Nazi pressure.

Sources: Cantor's Paradise – meeting Hitler 1933 | Britannica – Nazi period

The hardest blow came in January 1945. His son Erwin Planck, former officer and moderate politician, was arrested for alleged participation in 20 July plot to assassinate Hitler and executed on 23 January 1945 in Plötzensee prison. Planck, then 86, whose house had been destroyed in 1944 bombing, wrote in a letter: “I suffer the greatest pain of my life.” He died two years later, on 4 October 1947 in Göttingen and was buried there.

Sources: Historydraft – Erwin executed 23 Jan 1945 | Britannica – house destroyed 1944 death 1947

Stories People Tell — Marked “No Official Document”

One of most famous stories is that von Jolly told young Planck physics was complete and he should switch fields. This story appears in memoirs of many physicists and even textbooks, but no letter from Jolly with this exact sentence has been found. It seems Jolly was indeed pessimistic about future of physics, but exact phrase “physics is complete” is more an oral reconstruction from students’ memories than a documented quote.

This anecdote appears in students' memoirs but no contemporary letter with exact wording exists — oral tradition.
Source: Britannica – von Jolly anecdote

Another quote famous is Planck’s: “A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents, but rather its opponents eventually die and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it.” This sentence appears with slight differences in his Scientific Autobiography in 1949 and became known as Planck’s principle. Sociologists of science today see it as exaggerated, but its core is true: acceptance of revolutionary ideas often takes a generation.

Source: Wikiquote – Scientific Autobiography 1949

Reflection Today

🔬 When SI was redefined in 2019, kilogram was no longer defined by a metal cylinder, but by Planck constant h. That means from then on, every scale in world indirectly works with Planck’s 1900 discovery. This may be his greatest practical legacy: turning a laboratory constant into a basis for measuring the world.
Sources: CODATA – SI 2019 redefinition | Max Planck Society – legacy

Legacy in Your Pocket

If you use your phone today, you owe Planck. Transistor that is heart of phone cannot be explained without quantum, laser that builds fiber-optic internet uses quantized transitions first described by Planck, and atomic clock that makes GPS accurate works with frequencies that are multiples of h. Even infrared medical thermometers and climate models predicting global warming use Planck’s radiation law. Max Planck Society today with 83 institutes in Germany and world continues same spirit of “science for knowledge” that Planck devoted his life to.

Four Common Misunderstandings

1. Planck loved quantum from start. No, he called it “act of desperation” and for years hoped to return it to classical physics. It was Einstein who took quantum seriously.

2. Planck was anti-religious. No, he was church elder and believed religion and science complement, but did not believe in personal God.

3. Collaborated with Nazis. No, he stayed to protect scientific institutions, met Hitler for Jews and was rejected, and resigned 1937.

4. Measured Planck constant precisely. Initial value was approximate; exact modern value comes from SI definition 2019.

Sources: Klein 1962 | MPG Archive | Cantor | CODATA

Summary: Man Who Saw World in Packets

Max Planck was born 23 April 1858 in Kiel, split physics with a seemingly small assumption on 14 December 1900, won Nobel 1918, stayed in Germany’s hardest years to defend science, lost four children and his house, and died 4 October 1947 in Göttingen. His small constant h today lives in every chip, every laser, every atomic clock, reminding us that sometimes biggest revolutions come from most conservative minds.
