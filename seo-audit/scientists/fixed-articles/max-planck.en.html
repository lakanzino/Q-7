Max Planck (1858–1947) — The Reluctant Father of Quantum Theory

Key takeaways: Max Planck biography is the story of a conservative German professor who, in December 1900, introduced energy quanta to solve blackbody radiation and accidentally launched quantum mechanics. Born 23 April 1858 in Kiel, died 4 October 1947 in Göttingen, Planck won the Nobel Prize in Physics 1918 for his discovery of energy quanta [Nobel Prize 1918]. He mentored Heisenberg and Pauli, served as president of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society, and endured immense personal tragedy — losing his first wife, three children, and seeing his son Erwin executed by the Gestapo in 1945 [Britannica]. This article traces his life, science, faith, and ethics with inline sources.

Max Planck did not want a revolution. He wanted a formula that fit the data. Yet the formula forced him to say something no physicist had dared: energy is not continuous. It comes in packets. If you understand why that idea shocked even Planck himself, you understand why quantum physics still feels strange today. Early Life and Family — A Scholarly Household

Max Planck was born on 23 April 1858 in Kiel, then part of the Duchy of Holstein, into a family where church, law, and university were everyday words [Britannica]. His father, Johann Julius Wilhelm Planck, was a professor of law at Kiel and later Munich; his great-grandfather and grandfather were theology professors at Göttingen [Wikipedia]. That tradition — devotion to duty, conservatism, incorruptibility, and generosity — shaped Planck for life [Britannica].

When Planck was nine, his father received a call to Munich, and the family moved. Max entered the prestigious Maximilian Gymnasium, where teacher Hermann Müller sparked his interest in physics and mathematics and taught him astronomy and mechanics as well as the principle of conservation of energy [Britannica]. He was also a gifted pianist and organist, and for a time hesitated between music and physics. A family friend told him physics was almost finished, with only a few gaps to fill — an anecdote often repeated, though its exact wording has no official transcript — This story is widely told by students, but no contemporary document confirms the exact phrase; it survives as oral tradition.

Planck's baptismal name was Karl Ernst Ludwig Marx Planck; Marx was the appellation name. By age ten he signed as Max and kept it [Wikipedia]. That small act of self-naming already showed the quiet independence that would later let him challenge classical physics.

Education — Munich and Berlin

Planck entered the University of Munich in fall 1874 at age 16. Physics professor Philipp von Jolly did not encourage him, but Planck found his path through independent study of Rudolf Clausius on thermodynamics [Britannica]. In 1877–1878 he spent a year at the University of Berlin, hearing Hermann von Helmholtz and Gustav Kirchhoff — eminent, but whose lectures he found uninspiring [Britannica]. He learned more from their writings and from self-study.

He received his doctorate in July 1879 at age 21 with a thesis Über den zweiten Hauptsatz der mechanischen Wärmetheorie (On the Second Law of Thermodynamics) [Wikipedia]. His habilitation followed in 1880 in Munich, then an unpaid Privatdozent position. He worked on thermodynamics, rediscovering results of Josiah Willard Gibbs without knowing it — a sign of how isolated German thermodynamics was at the time [Klein 1962].

In 1885 he became professor of theoretical physics at Kiel, and in 1889 succeeded Kirchhoff in Berlin, where he would teach until 1928. The plaque at Humboldt University still reads: “Max Planck, discoverer of the elementary quantum of action h, taught in this building from 1889 to 1928” [Humboldt University].

Career Path — From Thermodynamics to Quantum

Planck's early career was devoted to the second law and entropy. In 1894 he turned to a problem that seemed technical: blackbody radiation — the spectrum of light emitted by a perfect absorber. Experimentalists at the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt in Berlin, Rubens and Kurlbaum, had precise measurements showing that Wien's law failed at long wavelengths [Klein 1962]. Planck was informed of their results days before they were presented on 19 October 1900, and prepared an interpolation formula that fit both Wien at short wavelengths and Rayleigh at long wavelengths [Klein 1962].

On 19 October 1900 he presented that formula. It worked, but he had no derivation. In the following weeks he tried to derive it from entropy and Boltzmann's statistical ideas — which he had long resisted. On 14 December 1900, at the German Physical Society, he presented the derivation that introduced energy quanta: E = nhν [Planck 1901]. The date is now celebrated as the birth of quantum theory [Nobel Prize 1918].

Major Scientific Breakthroughs

1. Solving the Blackbody Problem — 14 December 1900

Classical physics predicted the Rayleigh-Jeans law, which diverged at short wavelengths — the ultraviolet catastrophe. Planck proposed that oscillators in the cavity wall can only have energies that are integer multiples of hν, where h is a new universal constant and ν is frequency [Planck 1901]. Then the average energy per mode becomes hν / (exp(hν/kT)-1), not kT, and the spectral energy density becomes u(ν,T) = (8πhν³/c³) / (exp(hν/kT)-1) [Britannica]. This Planck's law matched experiments across all wavelengths [CODATA].

He called it an act of desperation. In letters he later wrote that he introduced quanta as a purely formal assumption, not a physical reality [Kuhn 1978]. It was Albert Einstein in 1905 who took quanta literally to explain the photoelectric effect [Einstein 1905]. Planck himself remained cautious for years.

2. Planck Constant h — The Smallest Action

Planck constant h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s is now exact by definition since the 2019 SI redefinition, which defines the kilogram via h [CODATA 2019]. It is the quantum of action. Planck also introduced the Boltzmann constant k = 1.380649×10⁻²³ J/K, computed from his radiation data: h = 6.55×10⁻²⁷ erg·s and k = 1.346×10⁻¹⁶ erg/K in his first estimate [Klein 1962]. From h, c, and G he defined natural units — Planck length, mass, time — that are independent of human artifacts [Wikipedia].

3. Champion of Relativity and Mentor

Planck was an early champion of Einstein's special relativity in 1905–1906, organizing colloquia and defending it when many were skeptical [Britannica]. His Berlin lectures attracted a generation: Max von Laue, Walther Bothe, Walther Meissner, and later Werner Heisenberg and Wolfgang Pauli, both Nobel laureates [Britannica]. He served as rector of Berlin University in 1913–1914 and president of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society 1930–1937 [Wikipedia].

Personal Life, Beliefs, and Ethics

Planck married Marie Merck in 1887; they had four children: Karl (1888–1916), twins Emma (1889–1919) and Grete (1889–1917), and Erwin (1893–1945) [Wikidoc]. Marie died in 1909, possibly from tuberculosis [Wikidoc]. In 1911 he married Marga von Hoesslin; their son Hermann was born in December 1911 [Wikidoc].

What was he known for ethically? Contemporaries described Planck as incorruptible, conservative, idealistic, reliable, and generous — values from his family's long tradition of church and state service [Britannica]. He loved music, played piano at a high level, and hosted musical evenings with Einstein and others. He was known for duty and stoicism: after losing his wife, his oldest son Karl killed at Verdun in 1916, daughter Grete dying in childbirth in 1917, daughter Emma dying the same way in 1919 after marrying Grete's widower, he endured stoically [Wikidoc]. Both granddaughters survived and were named after their mothers [Wikidoc].

Religion: Planck was raised Lutheran and served as a church elder (Kirchenältester) in Berlin — a role akin to assistant pastor, involving prayer and pastoral care [Enlightened Crowd]. Yet his writings show evolution. In Religion und Naturwissenschaft (1937) he wrote: “There can never be any real opposition between religion and science; for the one is the complement of the other” [Wikiquote]. In 1947, months before death, he wrote to W. H. Kick: “I have always been deeply religious, but I do not believe in a personal, let alone Christian God” [Archiv MPG][StackExchange]. Historians describe him as a deist who saw God as order, not as personal revelation [Forum Phil]. He argued science needs faith in intelligibility, and religion needs respect for causation: “Religion belongs to that realm that is inviolable before the law of causation and therefore closed to science” [Vatican Observatory].

Nazi period — moral complexity: Planck remained in Germany when many left. In spring 1933 he personally met Hitler to plead for Jewish colleagues — the attempt failed and Hitler flew into fury [Cantor's Paradise]. He publicly defended Einstein and Fritz Haber, and as president of Kaiser Wilhelm Society tried to protect the institute, resigning in 1937 when pressured [EBSCO]. He was criticized for not protesting more loudly, but also for using his prestige to help individuals. His house in Berlin was destroyed by Allied bombs in 1944, losing his library and correspondence [Cantor's Paradise].

The deepest wound came in 1944–1945. His son Erwin, to whom he was particularly close, was implicated in the 20 July plot to assassinate Hitler [Historydraft]. Tried by the Volksgerichtshof, Erwin was sentenced to death and hanged at Plötzensee Prison on 23 January 1945 [Historydraft]. Planck wrote to a friend: “You give me credit for too much if you think I have the strength to withstand this pain” [IOP]. He died two years later, on 4 October 1947 in Göttingen from a stroke [Britannica].

Stories People Tell — With Disclaimer

They say that in 1874, young Planck asked von Jolly whether to pursue physics, and von Jolly replied physics was nearly complete, only a few gaps remain — This anecdote is told in many biographies, but no contemporary letter confirms the exact wording; it survives as oral tradition from Planck's later recollections.

Another story: Planck once said, “A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents, but rather because its opponents eventually die” — often quoted as Planck's principle. The quote appears in his Scientific Autobiography 1949, but in a more nuanced form about generational change [Wikiquote]. It is used today in discussions of paradigm shifts.

People also tell that Einstein played violin while Planck played piano at Planck's house — This is documented by multiple witnesses, including letters, and is considered reliable, though no recording exists.

Legacy and Impact on Today's Technology

Without Planck's h there is no transistor, no laser, no LED, no MRI, no quantum computing. Planck's law is used daily in infrared thermometers, climate modeling, and astrophysics to infer stellar temperatures. The Planck units define the scale where quantum gravity must appear. The Max Planck Society with 83 institutes is Germany's premier research organization [Max Planck Society]. When you use GPS, you use atomic clocks that rely on quantized transitions first glimpsed in Planck's work.

Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: Planck loved quanta from the start. No, he resisted physical interpretation for years and called it an act of desperation [Kuhn 1978].

Misconception 2: Planck was anti-religious. No, he was church elder, wrote extensively on religion and science as complementary [Wikiquote], though late letters show deist doubts [Archiv MPG].

Misconception 3: Planck collaborated with Nazis. No, he met Hitler to protect Jewish scientists, was rebuffed, and lost his son to Nazi execution [EBSCO][Cantor's Paradise]. Remaining was a moral choice debated by historians.

Misconception 4: Planck constant was measured by Planck precisely. His first value 6.55×10⁻²⁷ erg·s was approximate; modern exact value defined by SI 2019 [CODATA 2019].

Summary

Max Planck, born 23 April 1858 in Kiel, introduced energy quanta on 14 December 1900 to explain blackbody radiation, defining h and launching quantum theory. He won Nobel 1918, led German science through two world wars, endured profound personal loss, and died 4 October 1947 in Göttingen. His constant underpins modern technology from transistors to quantum computers. He was Lutheran, conservative, generous, and known for integrity and duty [Britannica][Nobel 1918].

Frequently Asked Questions

Who was Max Planck and why is he famous?
Max Planck was a German theoretical physicist who in 1900 proposed that energy comes in discrete packets called quanta, introducing Planck constant h and founding quantum theory, for which he won Nobel 1918 [Nobel 1918].

When did Max Planck introduce quantum theory?
14 December 1900 at the German Physical Society in Berlin, presenting his derivation of blackbody radiation law with E = hν [Planck 1901].

What is Planck constant?
h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s, the quantum of action, now exact by SI definition 2019, linking energy and frequency via E = hν [CODATA 2019].

Was Max Planck religious?
Raised Lutheran and church elder, he saw science and religion as complementary, not opposed, writing “There can never be any real opposition” [Wikiquote]. Late letters show deist doubts about personal God [Archiv MPG].

What personal tragedies did Planck face?
First wife died 1909, son Karl killed 1916, daughters Grete 1917 and Emma 1919 died in childbirth, son Erwin executed by Gestapo 23 Jan 1945, house destroyed 1944 [Wikidoc][Historydraft].

What is Max Planck's legacy today?
Max Planck Society with 83 institutes, Planck units, and foundation for all quantum technologies from lasers to quantum computing [Max Planck Society].
